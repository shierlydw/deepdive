Research

Design is the search for a good fit between a form, and it's context.

What makes a problem what it is: human needs, technical constraints, conflicting requirements, habits, edge cases, and relationships. Bad design appears where those forces remain unresolved. Good design appears where those misfits have been worked through carefully. Christopher Alexander, Notes on the Synthesis of Form (1964), via Karri Saarinen, 2026

In here, my aim is to first shape the problem. Before I can work on the form, I must know the fit.

Executive Summary

Elderly parents' digital banking struggles

From forgetting minimum balances to password confusion - digital tools fail elderly parents, creating ongoing friction in family financial support.

Security drives channel selection

Participants actively choose channels that limit exposure - eWallets as buffers, avoiding cash for elderly parents.

Multi-channel financial fragmentation

Participants manage numerous institutions simultaneously. Fragmentation is accepted as necessary - not a problem to solve.

High inertia without clear incentives

"It must not cause me additional steps." They won't switch without clear monetary incentives or convenience gains.

Dual transfer: scheduled + ad-hoc

Regular scheduled transfers combined with ad-hoc payments - a universal mental model for family money flows.

Gradual financial independence

Participants deliberately structure support to build independence - transitioning from joint to individual accounts over time.

Solution

Selected Solution

Trusted Contact Person (Safety Net)

As a proxy to help elderly, guard activities, or be reachable. A caretaker adult nominates a trusted contact to receive alerts about suspicious transactions on an elderly parent's account - oversight without removing autonomy.

Why I selected this solution

  • ·I chose the elderly segment, where the unmet gap is widest.
  • ·The bank has invested well in the young and digitally savvy. Youth savings accounts, allowance tools, shared savings pots, bill splitting, expense tracking. The day-to-day money management space is well served.
  • ·Malaysian banks do offer security features. Fund locking, account kill switches, transfer limits, ATM cash-out controls. These are useful, but they all assume one thing: that the account holder can act on their own. For an elderly user who forgets a password, misses a minimum balance, or gets talked into a transfer, self-protection is not enough.
  • ·None of these features involve a second, consented person. That is the gap.

Context Problem Solution Research Outcome

Streamlining login and registration steps to improve completion rate

Company MetLife Bangladesh
Role Design
Product strategy
User research
Platform Mobile app
Shipped Q3, 2024

Context

One by MetLife is an insurance app used to manage their policies, make payments, and access health services like doctor consultations. In 2024, the app went through a significant platform upgrade that required every existing user to log in again under a new security standard.

For many users, this was the first time they had to actively think about their login credentials.

The goal was to design a registration and login experience that felt straightforward for three different types of users, while making sure as few people as possible got lost along the way.

Problem

Problem & pain-points

Most users do not remember their username, policy number, and password

Session timeouts were reduced from 365 days to 15 minutes, and many users across all three account types had never actively memorised their login credentials.

Users moved through a series of inputs without a sense of what they were working toward

The flow did not clearly indicate which step was creating the account, and the sequence of inputs was not optimised. Some fields, like location, added length without being essential to account creation.

Existing user flow

Users are directed to explore as a guest, reducing the urgency to create an account

Displayed as a drawer sheet, the registration and login page are easy to dismiss, reinforcing the idea that having an account is optional.

The copy “Username (Mobile No.)” creates confusion

A small detail in the label created an unintended assumption. Calling the field “Username (Mobile No.)” suggested to some users that a policy tied to their mobile number was needed to register, when in fact it was not.

The previous UI prioritised feature discovery over user registration.

Solution

Shaping the solution

1

A more direct path to account creation

Instead of opening into a guest homepage, the app now asks users upfront whether they are new or already have an account. From there, registration starts with one simple question about their policy type, which puts each user on the right path from the beginning.

OTP verification was moved to later in the flow, so users are not asked to verify before they have even entered their details. Password creation was also reworded to Account creation, because the real message is that completing this step means the app remembers you and gets better the more you use it.

The end result was one cleaner flow that worked for all three user types, without asking anyone for more than they needed to give.

The new flow starts with intent rather than exploration, routing each user type through only the steps they need.

2

Choosing a login method

I explored several login methods including OTP, mobile number, and email. OTP was appealing for its simplicity but could not be applied universally as some individual policyholders had multiple or mismatched phone records. Letting users choose their own verification channel was also considered, but most users only had a mobile number on record, making the extra step unnecessary.

The final approach used email or policy number as the username, giving each user a credential they were more likely to already know.

Several explorations on login method

3

Identifying the account type

One of the first steps in registration required users to identify which type of account they were creating. Working with the UX writer, we explored different ways to present the options. Displaying answers as full-width buttons felt wordy and harder to scan. Simplifying to a yes or no question was cleaner but introduced an extra step in the flow. A radio list with a pre-selected default was the clearest approach, making it easy to read and quick to confirm before moving on.

Registration step to identify user type

Research

Research findings

Conducted an unmoderated prototype usability test with UX Army, with 20 participants.

Findings show that time on task is 3 minutes. Drop-off points occur during password creation, with the CTA button hidden by the keypad. Satisfaction scores averaged 4 out of 5 across testers, with the majority finding the questions relevant and the flow straightforward to complete. Most users would rather skip the onboarding questionnaire that offers personalisation.

“All the questions were relevant. There were no unnecessary questions.”

“I did not realise my email would be a username, that is misleading to me.”

“OTP allows me to proceed automatically.”

Outcome

Solution and outcome

The redesigned experience showed measurable improvement across all three user types. Non-customer registration steps were reduced from 10 to 6, with account creation placed as an optional step after key credentials were collected rather than a decision point upfront. Completion rates improved across the board, and the migration also resolved a backend data conflict that had previously prevented certain policyholders from completing registration.

Completion rate

Non-customer registration81% → 92%
Customer registration20% → 63%
First-time login74% → 88%

Task duration

Non-customer registration1:59 → 2:15

Scaling Design System
to support Asia Expansion

Driving consistency across products and streamlining operations

01
Who?

MetLife in Asia has adopted a nimble approach to each market across the region and has successfully established a diversified geographical footprint in Asia that spans across nine markets Australia, Bangladesh, China, Japan, Korea, India, Malaysia, Nepal and Vietnam.

02
How?

Local teams have faced challenges shipping products where the UI interface did not meet the necessary user experience.

03
Why?

Our mission is to ensure a unified design language across mobile app products and web portals. The Regional CX team aims to enable local teams by setting up Design System library to host components and interface.

Scaling and Unifying
Asia Design System

Building a design system that drives design adoption, minimizing tech debt, and enhancing user experience across products.

My Role
Design System Lead, Product Owner
Responsible for standardization of design assets and their implementation quality
Facilitate organizational/culture change and mindset shift for design system adoption
Partner with tech lead to plan, coordinate and deploy new releases
Duration
2023 - 2024
Scope
Steered product outcome, implemented across markets in Australia, Bangladesh, Nepal, and South Korea.

Situation

The MetLife Design System emerged from a fragmented and diverse digital ecosystem, spread across local design teams and various web and mobile platforms.

UI Bugs
  • ·Components were not ready to be reused
  • ·Snowball effect of issues unresolved
  • ·QA team faced repeating tasks
  • ·Caused from insufficient handover brief to developers

Challenges

Components are fixed, inflexible

Legacy files and coded components did not meet the urgent need to cater dynamic content. The components were not built to scale for different teams.

Duplicates of Design Components

As design and product teams continue their tasks, the interface designed by local teams grew and so did the minor variants. Small increments in design foundations such as colours, spacing, and UI continue to grow in variants.

Longer Development Time

Components required high effort from development due to a lack of documentation and communication to design team. This contributed to more delay during testing as there were many repeated tasks.

Different Dev Environment for Different teams

There was no single unifying language that was used across each dev teams. (Flutter/ React JS/ Native / Web Components). Alignment was an ongoing process.

Poor Readability for Users

Several of the designs have faced feedback of not meeting legibility needs for users (font size and font colour issues). A more stringent governance on quality control was needed in design teams.
